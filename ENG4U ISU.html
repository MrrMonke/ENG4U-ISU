Journal Entry No. 2

Why I Chose The Illegal

I chose The Illegal by Lawrence Hill because the story combines action with meaningful social issues such as identity, discrimination, and perseverance. The idea of following a talented runner who is forced to fight for survival immediately caught my attention because it seemed both exciting and intriguing. I also wanted to read a novel that explores challenges people face around the world while showing the importance of resilience and hope. Before beginning the novel, I expected it to be an emotional story that would challenge my perspective and help me better understand the experiences of people who are forced to overcome injustice.

Journal Entry No. 3

Character Development — Keita Ali

First Third of the Novel

At the beginning of The Illegal, Lawrence Hill introduces Keita Ali as a determined, humble, and hardworking young man whose greatest passion is running. His dream is to become an Olympic marathon champion because he believes success could improve both his own future and the lives of his family. However, his life is shattered by political violence and discrimination against the Faloo people. Keita witnesses the murder of Deacon Andrews, loses his mother during the conflict, and watches his father suffer imprisonment and torture because of his journalism. These tragedies force him to mature quickly and transform running into more than just a sport, it becomes his way of coping with grief and holding onto hope. Establishing the immediate political danger and the severe threats he faces right at home in Zantoroland, Hill writes:

"If Keita stayed in Zantoroland, he would die. He was his father's son, and that in itself would be a death sentence" (Hill 48).

This quotation demonstrates the crushing reality that forces Keita to flee his homeland. My first impression of Keita is that he is incredibly resilient because, despite everything he loses, he refuses to give up on himself or his dreams.

Second Third of the Novel

As the novel progresses, Keita becomes more independent and cautious after escaping Zantoroland and living as an undocumented immigrant in Freedom State. Although he continues pursuing his dream of becoming a successful runner, his priority shifts from achieving athletic success to simply surviving each day. He faces racism, poverty, isolation, and the constant fear of being discovered by immigration authorities, yet he refuses to let these hardships change his compassionate nature. Rather than responding to injustice with hatred, he continues relying on discipline, perseverance, and hope. Capturing his underground existence and the constant paranoia of being hunted by the state, Hill notes how he must navigate a place where people are targeted simply for existing without papers, quoting:

"What are you doing here? Do you have your papers? Are you an illegal?" (Hill 238).

Although brief, this dialogue highlights the constant systemic hostility Keita faces in this middle phase. As I continued reading, I realized that his greatest strength is not his speed, but his ability to keep moving forward even when life gives him every reason to stop. Lawrence Hill suggests that resilience comes not from physical strength alone, but from choosing hope over despair.

Final Third of the Novel

By the end of the novel, Keita has grown into a resilient and mature young man who understands that true strength comes from perseverance rather than revenge. Although he continues carrying the pain of losing his family and homeland, he refuses to let those experiences define his future. During the novel's major conflict, Keita responds with courage, integrity, and determination instead of bitterness. He also learns that family is not only the people he was born with but also the people who choose to support him when he has nowhere else to turn. Facing the ultimate pressure to win a race to secure rescue funds and protect his loved ones, Hill emphasizes the high-stakes ticking clock of the climax:

"If he did not raise that money, he'd never see his sister again" (Hill 273).

Looking back at the beginning of the novel, I can clearly see how much Keita has changed. He begins as a young athlete chasing Olympic dreams, but finishes as someone who understands that preserving his humanity is a far greater achievement than winning any race. His journey demonstrates that resilience is not simply about surviving difficult circumstances, it is about refusing to let those circumstances take away your identity, hope, or compassion.

Journal Entry No. 4

Context Research

The Author

Lawrence Hill is a Canadian author, journalist, and essayist born in Newmarket, Ontario, in 1957. He grew up in a family deeply involved in civil rights and social justice. His father, Daniel G. Hill, was a historian and human rights activist, while his mother, Donna Hill, also worked to promote equality. These experiences influenced many of Hill's novels, which often explore racism, identity, migration, and belonging. Hill has said he wanted The Illegal to focus on the personal experiences of refugees rather than political debates.

Understanding Hill's background helps me see why Keita's story focuses so much on identity, discrimination, and resilience. Knowing the author's personal interests makes the novel's message feel more authentic and purposeful.

Historical and Social Context

Although The Illegal is set in fictional countries, it reflects real issues that were affecting millions of people around the world when Hill wrote the novel. Refugee crises caused by war, political persecution, and violence displaced millions of people from countries such as Syria, South Sudan, and Eritrea. The novel also explores racism, human trafficking, poverty, and the challenges faced by undocumented immigrants.

Learning about these real-world events helped me realize that Keita's experiences are not unique to fiction. His struggles represent the challenges faced by many refugees today, making the novel much more realistic and meaningful.

The Novel's Setting

The novel begins in the fictional country of Zantoroland before Keita escapes to Freedom State. Although both countries are fictional, they reflect real societies affected by political instability, discrimination, and unequal treatment of minority groups. Even after reaching Freedom State, Keita continues to face fear, exploitation, and the threat of deportation because of his immigration status.

Understanding why Lawrence Hill created fictional countries helped me realize that the novel is not criticizing one nation in particular. Instead, it shows that prejudice and injustice can exist anywhere, making Keita's story feel universal rather than tied to one specific place.

Journal Entry No. 5

The Theme of Resilience

Throughout The Illegal, Lawrence Hill presents resilience as the ability to keep moving forward despite overwhelming loss and injustice. Keita Ali demonstrates this right from the start when his world falls apart due to political violence and the loss of his family; instead of giving up, Hill notes how he turns to his passion as a vital source of strength:

"When his father did not know where to put his pain, he wrote. Charity studied. Keita went running." (Hill 29).

As an undocumented refugee in Freedom State, Keita faces poverty, racism, and constant fear, yet he refuses to surrender. Hill emphasizes this enduring drive, noting how

"He ran because he had to. He ran to survive" (Hill 154).

Other characters also display resilience, such as Charity continuing her education despite the danger and compassionate allies helping Keita against the odds. This proves that resilience is strengthened not just by inner determination, but through human support. Ultimately, Hill suggests that true resilience is about preserving your humanity and hope rather than just avoiding suffering. Facing the ultimate test to save his sister, Keita's endurance is driven by a profound sense of purpose:

"If he did not raise that money, he'd never see his sister again" (Hill 273).

Through his journey, Lawrence Hill communicates that resilience is one of a person's greatest strengths, allowing individuals to overcome adversity without losing who they truly are.

Journal Entry No. 6

Literary Lens — Socio-Economic

Applying a socio-economic lens to The Illegal by Lawrence Hill deeply transforms the interpretation of the narrative by framing Keita Ali's struggles not merely as personal hardships, but as the direct result of systemic inequality, wealth disparity, and corrupt political power structures. From this perspective, the wealthy nation of Freedom State and the impoverished island of Zantoroland represent a stark global divide where institutional forces systematically strip marginalized populations of their basic human rights and legal identity. Keita's desperate flight across borders highlights how class and economic status dictate survival, turning vulnerable individuals into "illegals" who are exploited for cheap labor while being denied societal protection. This systemic oppression is powerfully illustrated when Keita navigates an environment where his very existence is criminalized by bureaucracy, prompting him to recognize that

"What are you doing here? Do you have your papers? Are you an illegal?" (Hill 238).

Ultimately, viewing the novel through a socio-economic lens shifts the core message from a simple tale of individual athletic perseverance to a sharp critique of global capitalism and political corruption, revealing that true justice requires challenging the power structures that treat human lives as disposable commodities.

Journal Entry No. 7

Text-to-Text Connections

What the two texts have in common (Themes, characters, conflicts, messages):

Both The Illegal and the film The Pursuit of Happyness center on resilient, dedicated individuals facing overwhelming socio-economic hardships, poverty, and displacement while trying to secure a safe, stable future for their families.

Just as Keita Ali is hunted as an undocumented refugee in Freedom State while clinging to his passion and survival, Chris Gardner faces homelessness, extreme financial ruin, and systemic barriers in San Francisco while working relentlessly to build a better life for his son. Both stories share powerful themes of unyielding perseverance and human dignity.

What makes them different:

The origins of their conflicts differ: Keita's struggle is driven by geopolitical corruption and immigration laws that render him invisible, whereas Chris's struggles are rooted in domestic urban poverty and corporate precarity.

Their primary vehicles for survival also differ: Keita relies on elite marathon running, while Chris relies on salesmanship and stock brokerage training.

How the comparison helps you better understand your novel:

Comparing these two texts helps us better understand The Illegal by showing that Keita's relentless running is not just an athletic pursuit, but a profound symbol of human agency against an unjust global order that denies his basic right to exist.

Journal Entry No. 8

Creative Prompt Response

Chosen Prompt: "What if the very thing you used to achieve glory became the only thing keeping you alive?"

For Keita Ali, running begins as a pure expression of joy and a proud pursuit of Olympic glory on the island of Zantoroland. However, when political violence shatters his world and forces him into the shadows as an undocumented refugee in Freedom State, that same athletic passion undergoes a profound transformation. It is no longer about medals or podium finishes; it becomes a desperate, literal mechanism for survival. As Lawrence Hill illustrates through Keita's relentless underground races to secure funds and protect his family, running morphs into his only shield against an oppressive system that seeks to render him invisible. This shift demonstrates that Keita's greatest strength is not just his physical speed, but his refusal to let circumstances strip away the core identity and hope that define who he is.

glory field → survival street
Journal Entry No. 9

Creative Prompt Response

Chosen Prompt: "How does the setting, moving from the political instability of Zantoroland to the cold bureaucracy of Freedom State, shape the choices and moral dilemmas of the characters?"

The stark contrast between Zantoroland and Freedom State serves as a driving force behind every moral dilemma and survival choice the characters make. In Zantoroland, the constant threat of state-sanctioned violence and corruption forces characters like Keita's journalist father to risk everything to speak the truth, while forcing Keita into immediate flight. Upon arriving in Freedom State, a wealthy democracy built on exclusionary immigration laws, Keita faces a completely different kind of danger: systemic alienation, poverty, and the invisible cage of being labeled an "illegal." This environment forces otherwise law-abiding individuals, such as Ivernia Beech and supportive local journalists, to break the law and subvert bureaucratic rules just to extend basic human kindness. Ultimately, the setting highlights that systemic injustice forces ordinary people to choose between blindly following corrupt laws or maintaining their basic human empathy.
